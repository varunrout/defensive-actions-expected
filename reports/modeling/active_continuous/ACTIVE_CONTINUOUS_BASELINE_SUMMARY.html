1. Why a hurdle architecture, not a single regression

Two rough analyses (done directly in chat, cited here since they are not yet committed elsewhere in this repo) settled the two open questions this leg's design depends on.

Distribution shape. target_future_xg_10s's shot-conditional distribution (the 4,368 rows where a shot did follow) is heavily right-skewed (skew ~3.2-3.3, excess kurtosis ~13-14) and, critically, not bounded at 1 — reconfirmed directly against the real data: 7 of the 4,368 positive rows have target_future_xg_10s > 1 (max observed: 1.239). This happens because the target sums xG across every shot in the 10-second window, not one shot's probability. This single fact rules out beta regression outright and makes a single Tweedie regression across all rows a worse fit than treating "does a shot happen" and "how much xG, given it does" as two separate, differently-shaped problems. Log-normal was the best-fitting family among log-normal/gamma/weibull/beta for the positive-row distribution. This is why this leg is built as a two-part ("hurdle") model.

Window length — an accepted, documented limitation, not an open question. A separate sensitivity check (25-match sample, real target-building code, horizons 5/10/15/20/30s plus an uncapped possession-bound run) found the existing 10-second window captures only ~41% of possessions that eventually produce a shot (median time-to-shot, within a possession that does produce one: 14 seconds). Widening to a full possession-bound framing was checked and found worse: some possessions run 60-390+ seconds, well past where the original action's own features remain a fair predictor. Varun's explicit decision: keep the existing 10-second window. This baseline does not revisit that decision.

5. Two back-transforms: naive vs log-normal-corrected

Exponentiating a log-scale prediction directly (exp(pred)) recovers the median of the implied log-normal distribution, not its mean — and E[xg] is what the downstream hurdle combination actually needs. By Jensen's inequality, the naive back-transform systematically under-predicts the true conditional mean. The standard correction, exp(pred + σ²/2), fixes this.

Real numbers, c1, held-out test: fitted σ² = 0.934, giving a mean correction factor exp(0.934/2) ≈ 1.60.

Which one the hurdle combination should use, and why: the corrected back-transform. Naive exp(pred) has lower MAE (CV: 0.0721 vs 0.0823) — but MAE is minimized by the median, and naive exp(pred) is approximately a median-type estimator. R² and RMSE-type metrics, about matching the mean, tell the opposite story: naive R² is negative (-0.047), corrected R² is positive (+0.029). Since the hurdle combination needs an unbiased mean estimate, the corrected back-transform is used throughout, reported honestly alongside the naive numbers rather than only quoting whichever looks better.

8. Paired significance test, c1 vs c0

Common-scale log RMSE, per canonical CV fold: c1 beats c0 in 5 of 5 folds (mean diff -0.1394). Paired t-test t=-11.04, p=0.0004. Wilcoxon signed-rank p=0.0625, the same 5-fold floor seen throughout this project.

Read cautiously: this leg's per-fold sample (628-830 positive rows) is much smaller than the binary legs'. Reported plainly, not suppressed — directionally informative, not strong statistical proof on its own. 5/5 folds agreeing, plus the held-out readout confirming the same ranking, is a real, multi-angle-consistent signal.
